# windows-cpp: program structure, groups and pictures

**Status:** Built. How the plain target lays out objects, screens, groups, pictures and names; approach and open questions in [generator](40-generator.md).

- *SFML types are the objects.* An object is a global `sf::CircleShape`, `sf::RectangleShape`, `sf::Text` or `sf::Sprite` named as in the game, plus `<name>Velocity` if it moves, and a `start<Name>()` function if a rule resets it (sets its variables, drawing any `<random>` again, then its position and velocity); no struct, so nothing has to be complete before `main` and the objects sit between the tunables and the declarations. A text showing a `<number>` has a `show<Name>()` that sets its string and puts it in place by its new size, called wherever that number changes.
- *Screens are an enum and a stack.* With more than one `<state>`, `enum class Screen` and `std::vector<Screen> screens`, the one on top showing; `<push>`, `<pop>` and `<pop state>` are `push_back`, `pop_back` and assigning `back()`, and `<reset />` on a key or in a condition calls `start()`, which is the whole game from the start. One screen has neither.
- *A group is a `std::vector`* of its members' shapes, at the author's ask (not an array): `std::vector<sf::RectangleShape> debris1(3);`, with one `sf::Vector2f debris1Velocity` when the members share their group's velocity and a `std::vector<sf::Vector2f>` when any has its own. Its update, look and drawing are loops (`for (sf::RectangleShape& one : debris1)`, or by index when each has a velocity); its start places each member (`debris1[1].setPosition(...)`) from what it gives and its group gives. A rule about a group from another object is a loop over its members (`for (const sf::RectangleShape& other : debris1)`). A `<reset />` of one member calls `startBoulders(i)`, the start of one member: what every member shares written once, and what a member gives itself as a list of one for each (`const float xs[] = {50.0f, 230.0f, ...}; boulders[i].setPosition({xs[i], randomBetween(-500.0f, -40.0f)})`); `start()` calls it for each. A group that resets, or whose velocity draws a `<random>`, keeps a velocity for each member, since the engine draws one for each. A `switch` over the members was the other way, and repeats the whole start once for each. Cells, and groups in lockstep, are not reset one at a time yet.
- *`<die />` is a flag.* What can die (an object or group with a `<die />` in its rules) has `bool ballAlive`, or `std::vector<bool> bricksAlive` beside a group's shapes, set true in `start()`. It is drawn, moved by its keys, updated and touched only while the flag is set, as the engine's `die()` hides the object and turns off its collisions; dying ends the rest of its rules that frame (`return`, or `continue` to the next member, `break` out of a loop over another group's members). A group that can die is looped by index (`bricks[i]`, and `j` for another group's members) so the flag goes with the shape. Taking a dead member out of the vector was looked at and not chosen: `start()` would have to build it again, and the members' own looks and velocities are set by their place in it.
- *A group in columns and rows is its cells in the vector*, the top row first as the engine makes them. Columns and rows are whole numbers and the picks (`2 4`, `odd`) are fixed, so `groups.xsl` works out every cell when the program is written: the group's sprite, velocity and padding changed by the rows, columns and cell that pick it, in that order, as `readGroup` does, each look and velocity once. The program then says only the differences: the group's look on all of them in a loop, then each other look on the cells that have it, only what changed (`bricks[i].setFillColor(...)` for Breakout's rows), in a loop over a run of cells, whole rows, whole columns, or a list; a velocity each (`aliensVelocity.assign(...)`, then each change) only when a part gives one. Rows alike in size and gaps are two loops (`bricks[row * 9 + column].setPosition({margin + static_cast<float>(column) * (width + 5.0f), ...})`); rows that are not go one at a time down a running `bricksTop`, and a row whose gaps differ (a `<column>`'s padding) a cell at a time; a cell of another size than its row is moved to the middle of its place. Each look that is a picture is a texture of its own, named for what made it (`invadersRow2Picture`), sharing a bitmap's rows with a look that has the same ones. Writing the program to work the cells out as it starts (a table of rows and columns, and the merging in C++) was the other way, and was not chosen: the program would carry the engine's group reading, where a person writes the result. A member's sprite is worked out the same way, so a member may give only what changes. A group's or a row's `<variables>` wait for variables of a group's members.
- *`<lockstep>` is one move for the block.* A group in lockstep is all moved first, then its rules; a `<bounce />` off a side by any member turns the whole block (`aliensVelocity.x = -aliensVelocity.x`, and every member steps back), as the engine's `updateLockstepObjects`. Moving the block member by member, as other groups are, would have turned it with some members already moved and some not, and opened gaps.
- *`<remaining>` counts flags.* `std::count(bricksAlive.begin(), bricksAlive.end(), true)` for a group that can die, `ballAlive ? 1 : 0` for an object, a number for what cannot die; `!ballAlive` when it is objects alone and none may be left.
- *Pictures the game draws are drawn where a person would.* A `<bitmap>` is its rows written out in `main.cpp`, one under another so the code looks like the sprite (`const std::vector<std::string> playerRows = {...}`), and `<line>`s a `std::vector<pictures::Line>`; the module `pictures.h` turns them into an `sf::Image` when the program starts, a copy of the engine's `rasterizeRows` and `rasterizeLines` (`lib/source/bitmap.cpp`), so the pixels are the engine's. Keep the two in step. An `<svg>` is different: drawing one needs an SVG library, which a plain SFML program does not have, so xgecli draws it while generating, with the engine's own `rasterizeSvg`, into `assets/drawn/<name>.png` (a small PNG writer in `generate.cpp`, stored rather than compressed), and the program loads a picture. Taking lunasvg into the generated program was the other way, and was not chosen: a second dependency for what a person would export to a PNG once. Its numbers are worked out then, so they must be numbers or game variables that are. Each drawn sprite's texture is named for what shows it (`playerPicture`, `aliensPicture`), images keep theirs named for the file.
- *Looks are an enum and a function.* An object or group with several looks gets `enum class StrongLook { Whole, Cracked };`, a variable for the look it shows (a `std::vector` of them for a group) and `becomeStrong(i, look)`, which notes the look and sets the shape's fields for it in a `switch`; `start()` calls it with the first look. A row, column, cell or member that changes a look (Space Invaders' crabs and octopuses) makes a picture of its own for it, and `become` picks among them with an `if` on the index, by runs (`i >= 11 && i <= 32`), the group's own look in the `else`; one switch on the look, with the parts inside it, was chosen over a look per part, so a rule or an animation names the look and not the row. An `<animation>` is a list of its frames' looks, and for each member where it is and how many frames it has shown that look (`aliensFrame[i]`, `aliensShown[i]`); `animate<Name>()`, first in the screen's update as the engine moves animations first, counts up to the interval in frames and calls `become` with the next. A `sprite=` rule is an `if` on the variable, tested as the rule comes, so a rule that changes the look is seen by the next one, as in the engine (Breakout's top row: cracked goes, then whole cracks). Keeping one shape and setting its fields was chosen over a shape per look, which would need every rule and draw to pick one. On an edge the rules are one touch of it, so a `sprite=` rule is an `if` inside that touch; when a `<die />` sits under a look, the way out after the touch asks whether it died.
- *`<reset object>` is a function of the whole start.* What a `<reset object="...">` names gets `reset<Name>()`, its whole start (variables, place, velocity, in play or not, look, timers) and the numbers that show its variables; `start()` calls it rather than writing the start twice. An object no screen shows whose variables are used (Megamania's energy) keeps its variables.
- *A function per screen and per object.* `update<Screen>()` holds the keys held (a `<trigger>` of an action of `<move>`s, looked at every frame), calls the update of each object it shows that does something, then tests its conditions (any object the condition names or whose class it names, then its commands and `return`, since the screen may have changed). `update<Name>()` holds an object's move and its rules as `if` statements in the order written. Keys that do anything else are pressed, not held: `pressed()` gets each key press from the event loop, with a `switch` over the screen showing. `main`'s update part calls the showing screen's update; its render part draws that screen's objects.
- *Names in camel case* (`ball.radius` is `ballRadius`, `window.width.center` is `windowWidthCenter`), and a constant for each of the window's names the game uses. An object's size (`title.width`) is `physics::width(title)`, read as it is drawn now. A name C++, its library or the program already has gets an underscore (Pong's object `continue` is `continue_`, Air-Sea Battle's `clock` is `clock_`), and so does a game variable named like an object or group, the object keeping the plain name (Frostbite's `row1` is a group and a tunable, `row1_`). A name the program would not have is refused rather than guessed.
- *A member shown on its own is an object.* `prepare.xsl` runs on the game before the rest and makes a member that a screen shows by its own name, its group shown nowhere, an object: the group with the member's parts in place of its own, axis by axis for a position or a velocity (Pitfall!'s two scorpions, each under its screen). Writing the group as a vector with a flag for each member on each screen was the other way, and a person would write two objects. A second stylesheet run first, rather than a mode inside the first, because the rest of the stylesheets read the game from its root.
- *Physics and sound are modules of their own*, in `generators/windows-cpp/modules/` and copied beside `main.cpp` only when the game uses them, at the author's ask for "a minimum set of tools" a generated game includes rather than engine code. `physics.h` is header only, `namespace physics`: where a thing is (`left`, `width`, `touching`), whether it is `past` an edge of an area, what it does there (`bounce`, `stick`, `wrap`, `putBack`) or against another thing (`bounceOff`, `deflect`), and a `hop` that stays in the area; templates, so one function serves a shape, a sprite and a text. `sound.h` and `sound.cpp`, `namespace sound`: a sound is notes (wave, pitch, slide, length) made into samples at start, the same synthesizer as the engine's (`lib/source/sound.cpp`: the waves, the 2 ms fade, the even slide, the fixed noise), and `sound::Sound` holds the buffer and the voice. Each uses nothing but SFML, so it can be copied into any SFML 3 program; the namespaces keep them clear of a game's own names (Pong has tunables called `width` and `height`).
- *Assets and other helpers only when used.* The font (`assets/tuffy.ttf`) when there is a text, each picture once; xgecli copies them, and the program's CMake copies `assets/` beside the built program. Small helpers that are not physics or sound (`randomBetween`, `sign`) are plain C++ in `functions.xml` with the standard headers they need (`uses=`); the includes are worked out from them.
