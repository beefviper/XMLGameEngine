# Cards

**Status:** Deck, piles and a hand Built (2026-11-01) with Klondike; players' cards, wild cards and a card-playing computer Built (2026-11-02) with Crazy Eights; points, a dealer and bets Built (2026-11-02) with Blackjack; patterns of cards, scoring and odds Built (2026-11-03) with Texas Hold'em; tricks Built (2026-11-04) with Hearts; trumps, partners and bids Built (2026-11-05) with Spades; decks of single cards Open.

## What we looked for

- Asked for (Beef, 2026-11-01): "we definitely need to make a deck of cards a first class element", agreed as the next class after the board games, before backgammon (dice).
- Beef: the game should say **how the deck is built**: an Italian deck has coins, cups, swords and batons and only 40 cards; some games use two packs. And later, games like trading card games, where each card is its own (a picture and numbers), not a suit and a rank.
- What a card game needs that the board games do not: **many cards in one place** (a cell holds one piece), stacks that are **fanned** or squared, cards **face up or down**, **shuffling and dealing**, and moving **a run of cards** at once by rules about the card below.

## What we chose and why

- **Three things, kept apart:** a `<deck>` makes the cards; a `<pile>` on an object is a place that holds cards, with its rules; a `<hand />` object is the player's cursor that points at piles and carries cards between them. Piles and the hand know nothing about suits; suits times ranks is only the first way a deck is made, so a deck of single cards can come later with the same piles.
- **A card is an ordinary object** (`cards.1` to `cards.52`), with variables `suit` and `rank` (numbers from 1, in the order written) and two looks, `face` and `back`. So conditions, sounds and looks work on cards as on anything.
- **The game draws its suits, the engine draws the card.** Each `<suit>` gives its colour and its pip as bitmap rows; ranks are labels (`A`, `2`, ... `K`, or `F C R` for the Italian court) drawn in a small built-in pixel font. The engine lays out each face (rank and pip in two corners, a big pip in the middle) as a bitmap, so every window library draws it as it draws any bitmap. Writing 52 faces by hand was the alternative, and is what a deck of single cards will do.
- **Faces at full detail, from parts** (2026-11-01, Beef: "splurge a little... a lot of image data in the XML is fine as long as it's organized"): the game draws each part once, as rows: a suit's pip, corner pip and ace pip; a rank's corner index, its pips as a grid of characters (`*`, `v` turned, `@` the ace's), or a court `<picture>` drawn as its top half (`<half />`) and completed turned round. The engine only places them, so 52 faces come from 4 suits and 13 ranks, and a card is 96 by 140 pixels at scale 1 in an 880 by 840 window (2026-11-02, Beef: bigger, unscaled, a window that suits a 1080p screen without crowding the taskbar), so the longest column (six hidden, thirteen showing) still ends above the bottom. The pips are drawn as shapes and sampled down to rows, the court pictures drawn on a grid half again as fine as the first ones. The grid was chosen over pixel positions so a layout reads as the card looks; thirteen rows put both three and four pips down a side on it. Court pictures are shared by the suits, `*` taking the suit's colour. Added `color.gold` and `color.peach` for them.
- **Pile rules are data, like `<moves>`:** what a pile `<takes>` (`<one />`, `<samesuit />`, `<othercolor />`, `<up />`, `<down />`, and `<empty><rank>K</rank></empty>` for the first card), whether a `<run />` can be taken from it, whether a face-down card left on top turns up (`<showtop />`), and `faces="up|down"` for piles that turn every card one way. The stock's behaviour is two properties: `<deals into="waste">1</deals>` and `<refill from="waste" />`.
- **The deal is the piles':** `<deck pile="stock">` puts the shuffled deck in one pile, and each pile's `<dealt>N</dealt>` takes N from it, in rounds, as a dealer does. A group of piles can say `<dealt>1<more>1</more></dealt>` (one, then one more each), which is Klondike's staircase without writing seven piles. A new game (a full reset) shuffles and deals again.
- **The hand works like Checkers' picker:** `<take />`, `<lay />` (refused when the pile does not take the cards, so what follows does not happen), `<drop />` puts back, `<deal />` deals from the pile pointed at. `<lay pile="foundations" />` lays on the first of those piles that takes the cards (the right click sends a card home). Laying back on the pile the cards came from is always allowed: it is putting back.
- **Moving the hand:** `<hop>` on a hand goes left and right to the next pile in the row (piles at the same height), up and down through the face-up cards of a fanned pile and then to the row above or below; `<track />` points at the top card under the pointer, and held cards follow the pointer. So a press, drag and release with the mouse, and Space twice with the keys, are both a move. Moved by the mouse, the hand takes, deals and lays only with the pointer over a pile or a card (a click on the table must do nothing and make no sound); with the keys it always points at a pile.
- **Drawing order:** cards are drawn in pile order, held cards above them and the hand above all, by a drawing order the engine keeps on objects (`drawOrder`, 0 for everything else, which keeps the file's order).
- **Live number `cards`** on a pile: how many it holds, so "all 52 on the foundations" is an ordinary condition with `total="sum"`.

- **Players' cards are a pile of their own** (2026-11-02, Crazy Eights): a pile fanned across, `<sorted />`, squeezed into a `<width>`, and a hand that owns it (`<hand pile="mine" />`). Owning is what the game needs said once: the hand takes from that pile alone (any card, face down too: the computer's are face down, and it knows them), draws into it from a stock with no `into=`, and keeps pointing into it, the pointed card lifted so it shows whole. A hand of its own for the computer, so its pointer moves as a person's would.
- **Wild cards and a call:** `<takes>` gets `<matches />` (suit or rank, the rule of the whole shedding family) and `<wild>` ranks. Laying a wild card makes the pile wait for a `<call />`; the suit called is the suit of a card the caller points at in its own hand, so no suit picker was needed: keys and mouse already point at cards, and a player always calls a suit they hold anyway. (Strict rules let any suit be named; this is the one place the game is narrower, chosen for having no extra screen.) The pile's live numbers `calling` and `called` let conditions pass the turn to the caller and show the suit.
- **The turn is the game's,** not the engine's: a `turn` variable and looks on the two hands gate which keys do anything, as in Checkers. The computer's own actions are on F12, pressed only by `<think>`.
- **The computer is a rule of thumb, not a search** (`<think>` on a hand): the other side's cards are hidden, so looking ahead means guessing them. It plays the suit it holds most of (keeping its options), the higher card first, and an eight only when nothing else goes; calls the suit it holds most of; draws when nothing goes. Every half second a step: point, then press.
- **The stock refills from the discards** with `<refill><shuffle /><leave>1</leave></refill>`: Klondike's turning over and Crazy Eights' reshuffle are one property.

- **Points are the deck's and the pile's** (Blackjack): what a card is worth is the game's deck (`<worth>` on a rank, more than one when it may count either), and how a pile adds them up is the pile's (`<points><atmost>21</atmost>`: every card that may count higher does while the total stays within). Only face-up cards count, so the dealer's shows what you can see. Asked of a pile as a live number, so conditions compare (`<above>dealer.points</above>`) and texts show it.
- **The dealer is conditions and a timer**, no new verb: while the table's phase says it plays and its points are under 17, a condition gives its hand the look a draw timer follows. The game deals through two hands with piles of their own (`<deal pile="stock" />`, `faces="down"` for the hole card), turns the hole card with `<reveal pile>` and gathers everything with `<shuffle pile>`. Bets and payouts are plain variables; the rules of settling are an ordered list of conditions.
- **The card a hand points at lifts** only on a pile that says so (`<lift>`): Blackjack's hands point at nothing anyone sees.

- **Patterns, not poker** (2026-11-03): the first Hold'em had a `<poker>` element, the engine ranking poker hands itself. Poker is a game, not a mechanic: a tag for it would invite a tag for every game. So the hands are written in the game as a `<patterns>` set from three mechanics, `<same>` (cards of a rank), `<run>` (worths in a row) and `<suited>` (cards of a suit), the same three that rummy's sets and runs and dice scoring are made of; the engine knows only how to find the best of them in some cards, and how to order two. Ace high and low is the ace's two `<worth>`s, Blackjack's word. Ties are one general rule (pattern, then a run from its top, else bigger groups first, then by worth), which is poker's and should suit most others.
- **Pile, not deck:** a pile `<score>`s by a named set, `<with>` a shared pile, the `<best>` so many: the same deck can be scored by different patterns in different piles.
- **Looked at and left:** each pattern spelling out its own tie-break (poker's rule is the common one); `<same>N` as exactly N: it is at least N, and the highest pattern made wins, which is what makes that right.
- **Odds, not a search, for the computer:** its opponent's cards are hidden, so it estimates how often its cards win against as many unseen ones, dealing what is still to come at random a thousand times (Monte Carlo). The random numbers are seeded by the cards seen, so the same cards always give the same odds: tests and replays stay fixed. Scoring is done once a frame, before the conditions, for piles whose cards changed: a deal of three cards scores once. Its play is four `<computer>` conditions: raise with odds of 65 or better, call when the odds beat the pot odds (`owed * 100 / (pot + owed)`), all in when short, else fold. Looked at and left: a bluffing or opponent-reading player; a full search over hidden cards.
- **Betting is the game's**, plain variables (pot, owed, raises, acted, street, turn) and an ordered list of conditions, as Blackjack's bets: no betting verbs. Space between hands shuffles, and a dealer's short timer then deals, so the stock can be stacked in between (tests).

- **A trick is a pile with a rule** (2026-11-04, Hearts): `<takes><follow />` (follow the suit led if you hold it) and `<breaks>` (suits that may not lead until one has been played) are the rules of the whole trick-taking family (Whist, Spades, Bridge, Euchre), not Hearts'. The pile knows who laid each card by the hand's `<seat>`, and says `lead` and `winner` as live numbers, so whose turn it is and who takes the trick are the game's conditions. Trumps came with Spades (2026-11-05): `<trumps>` beside `<breaks>`, since they change only who wins.
- **Points by card, not by rank worth:** `<points><each suit rank>` counts the pile's cards (a heart 1, the queen of spades 13). A rank's `<worth>` is what a card is worth in play; points by card are what it costs to take, so they stay on the pile.
- **Taken tricks go into a pile** with `<collect pile into>`, face down, out of play until the next deal; their points go to the taker by a condition reading `trick.points` before the collect. Shooting the moon is two conditions on the scores. `<deal>N</deal>` deals a whole hand at once.
- **The computer keeps out of tricks** with `<think><avoid /></think>`: the same rule-of-thumb player as Crazy Eights, with the one choice every trick game makes (try to take it, or not). Ducking with the highest card that still loses, shedding the most points when void. Looked at and left: counting cards played, which a later game may want.
- **Bids from a reckoning, partners from a team** (2026-11-05, Spades): a bid is a number the game keeps, so the only thing the engine adds is what a player counts to make it: the live number `tricks` on a hand's own pile, a fixed rule of thumb (high cards, trump length, short suits) as books on bidding teach it. The computers' bids are a `<set>` with `<largest>` of it and 1. A `<team>` on a hand is for the computer only: it lets a partner's safe winner stand; the game's sides are its own variables. Looked at and left: nil bids, a computer that bids from its partner's bid, counting played cards.

## Looked at and left

- **Cards as pieces on a map of cells:** a fanned column could be cells, but a stock or a foundation holds many cards in one place, and a run moves as one.
- **A key per pile** (press 1 to 7): not the key convention, and the mouse needs pointing anyway.
- **Turning the top card up by a press**: most computer versions of Klondike turn it at once; `<showtop />` says that.

## Open

- A deck of single cards (each its own picture and variables), for games where every card is different.
- A pass when no card goes and nothing is left to draw (every card but the top in hands: a turn then cannot end). A suit picker, if a game needs a suit called that no player holds.
- Hold'em with more than two players, no-limit betting, side pots, blinds instead of antes, a computer that bluffs.
- Blackjack's doubling down, splitting pairs, insurance, a shoe of several packs, buttons for the mouse.
- Scoring Crazy Eights across games (points for the cards left in the loser's hand), more than one computer player.
- Dealing three at a time from the stock, undo, scoring, an automatic finish.
- Hearts' passing of three cards before each hand, the two of clubs leading the first trick (the first leader goes round instead), no points on the first trick; nil bids and blind bids for Spades; a computer that counts cards.
