# Games as tests: games written by another AI

**Status:** Built.

## Games written by another AI (Berserk, Demon Attack, Frostbite)

The author asked another AI to write three games from `xgedef.xsd` alone. All three passed the schema and loaded, none could be played (a blank screen first). Every error was a command the schema accepts where the engine does nothing with it:

- **Colors** like `white` or `#00ff00` (a color is a `color.` name; anything else is transparent). **Key names** the engine lacks (`button="fire"` reads as `Unknown` and never runs).
- **`<move>` / `<fire>` in an `<input>`** (they only mean something in an object `<action>`), **`<inc>`/`<dec>` in a `<condition>`** and **`<reset object>` in a `<collision>`** (fixed later by [timers](09-timers.md)).
- **A variable with no owner** (`<inc variable="score">` does nothing; score and lives live on the player), **a spare projectile that starts enabled** (never fired; it flew off with nothing to put it away).
- Lesson: the vocabulary was enough (no tag was added), but the schema cannot tell an author a command is in the wrong place. Making the loader refuse unknown buttons and misplaced commands, and warn about unowned variables, would turn each mistake into a load error naming the line. **Done (2026-10-07)** at the author's request, as errors rather than warnings: unknown keys and colors, a command where it does nothing, and an `<inc>`/`<dec>` of a variable no object has each stop the load with what to write instead, and a misspelt name gets a "did you mean". Every shipped game still loads. Where a command works is a table in `load/expr.cpp` (`ruleFor`); `CommandExecutor::run` in `play/executor.cpp` does each verb with what its place gives it.
- Unknown colors are checked by a test that reads every `<color>` and `<background>`.

### Demon Attack (rewritten, 2026-10-10)

- The first one was seven rectangles bouncing in a 160 by 210 window. Rewritten from scratch like the other Atari games (640 by 480): a bitmap cannon, bombs (eight at most), and three waves as states: six flapping demons (two named bitmaps and an `<animation>`, each at a speed of its own, turning round and dropping bombs on random timers); four demons that `<release>` two small ones where they were shot, the wave ending when neither is left (they share a class); three big demons with two looks, `whole` and `hurt`, as Breakout's bricks have. `xge/tests/test_demonattack.cpp` plays it. Not in it: demons that dive at the cannon from the start, a colour for each wave. Tightened (2026-10-19, 623 to 506 lines): one-line sounds, shapes and timers, the bombs and small demons later became kit pieces with `<most>8</most>` (2026-10-23); nearer the 2600: an ice-planet ground the cannon sits on, the shot kept over the cannon as it flies (a laser timer that sets `laser.x` each frame, as the original steers it), and a spare cannon for every wave cleared, up to six.

### Frostbite

- Rewritten from scratch: a snowy shore and igloo along the top, four rows of drifting floes in alternating directions, Bailey jumping between rows with `<jump>`. Landing on a white row turns it blue (`<become>`) and adds an igloo block (`<reveal>`); four blue rows turn white again (a counting condition); water drowns unless on a floe; cold takes a degree a second (a state timer); geese push him along (`<ride />`); a fish is a bonus on a timer; the finished igloo opens, and walking in wins. Rewritten again (2026-10-19): three nights as [scenes](12-scenes.md) (a `from=` chain whose `<variables>` give the drift and what a floe is worth; the door goes the way `next`, so the ice is faster each night), the four rows one `<group>` of `<columns>` with white and blue bitmap looks and a `sprite="white"` rule that turns its row blue and triggers Bailey's `block` action, the igloo blocks waiting out of play, laid out a course at a time so `<reveal>` builds it bottom up, Bailey, geese and fish as multi-ink bitmaps; the temperature left is a bonus at the door; three nights home wins.

### Berserk (rewritten after Stern's Berzerk, 1980)

- Arcade facts: walls kill the man and robots; robots shoot once the score is high enough, only when lined up, and chase; he shoots in eight directions, one shot at a time; each room has exits in all four walls; next room is a different maze, entered from the opposite side; robot 50 points, 10 more each for clearing the room; Evil Otto (bouncing face, cannot be shot, passes walls) comes if you linger, slow while robots remain, as fast as the man after. (From web pages and a manual; the details not on the pages are least sure.)
- Built (rewritten 2026-10-19, 1167 to 697 lines): four rooms as the `<scenes>` of the playing state, each the arcade's frame of five cells by three (112 by 128, a 568 by 440 window) written as a `<map>` on half-cell tiles: `+` a pillar, `-` and `|` the walls between, `x` and `y` the exits in the middle of each side, never drawn. The first version's rooms were 12 by 8 cells of narrow corridors drawn as pixel bitmaps (a throwaway generator's), nothing like the arcade's open rooms, and once robots chased every one died on a wall within two seconds. Every exit leads by the way `out` to the next room, the fourth back to the first, and puts that room back as it started.
- The man has four looks (`<become>` in his actions) and `<facing>`, one shot in flight (one `bullet` object). The robots are one kit group with a member for each robot of each room (3, 4, 5, 6: `y1`..`y3`, `r1`.., `c1`.., `g1`..), each room taking its own by name and placing it; an inner group per room gives the colour, and all but the first room's yellow robots have an `<aim>` and `<fire>` timer, at most four shots in flight, whose wait is a `<random>` with ends using `player.depth`. Every 1 to 2.5 seconds (so they stand a while at first) a robot `<chase>`s the man a little faster the deeper he is, and one that touches a wall or another robot is destroyed, scoring as if shot. Otto is out of play until a room timer reveals him (14 seconds less depth, minimum 6, or at once when the room's robots are gone); one speed.
- Every rule that costs a life is the man's own (walls, shots, Otto, robots): a rule's `<reset />` puts only that object back, while `<reset object="player" />` in someone else's rule would reset his score and lives too (**`<reset object>` resets every variable**, not just position). `player.kills` counts the robots destroyed in the room; a state condition (`<all>` of no robot remaining and at least one kill) pays 10 for each, and the exit rule sets it back to 0. An extra man every 2000 points from `player.lifepoints`. Pause is P or Escape.
- Making it loadable found two slow spots in the engine, both fixed: the check that a value read while loading names nothing only known in play built an exprtk parser for every value (now once per distinct text; Pac-Man loads in 0.6 seconds instead of 4.4), and the pairs of objects that may touch asked each object whether it is in play once per pair instead of once.
- Differences: robots chase in a straight line rather than along rows and columns, the room layouts are fixed and only four (the arcade's are drawn from a seed), robots fire from the first visit to room two rather than once the score is high enough, Otto has two speeds (faster once the room is clear) and does not bounce. Speeds and waits were chosen without watching the game played: **tune them first.** Built 2026-10-28 (Beef asked, like the arcade): the man comes in at the side opposite the exit he took, and that doorway is shut behind him, electrified like the walls. Each gap has a class of its side (`northexit` ...), the man's rule for each sets his x and y and reveals a `doors` member just inside the opposite gap, and every exit resets the doors; no robot starts in a cell he comes in at. The four rooms are two by two and go round (each side its own way), where the arcade's are a far bigger grid of mazes worked out from each room's place; that grid is open (it needs maps made at run time). Also built 2026-10-28: eight directions (two keys held face the diagonal, and a shot follows it; engine rule for every `<facing>` object), Otto twice as fast once the room is clear (his `speed` variable, put back by his reset), and robots firing only when lined up (`<aim>` with `<lined>`, refused otherwise so the `<fire>` after it does not run).
